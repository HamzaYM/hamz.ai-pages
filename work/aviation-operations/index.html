A trip-feasibility engine for a business-aviation operator

A dispatcher used to spend hours per trip checking whether a flight was even possible, across a dozen disconnected systems. I built the engine that gathers that whole picture in one place, and kept a human in charge of every call it cannot make cleanly.

16 automated checks 5 AI-assisted, confidence-scored 10+ data sources unified hired and led the team
01The problem

Feasibility lived in a dozen places, and in one person's head

Before anyone could quote a private or charter trip, someone on the operations team had to assemble the entire feasibility picture by hand. Is the runway long and strong enough for this aircraft, fully loaded, wet? Are there NOTAMs closing anything? Are customs and immigration open at those hours? Is the crew inside its duty limits? Do we have permits to overfly and land?

A single trip meant hopping between maintenance records, flight planning, NOTAM feeds, security advisories, passport and visa data, and noise rules, then holding all of it in your head long enough to make a call. It was slow, it did not scale past the people who knew it cold, and the knowledge walked out the door at the end of the day.

02What I built

One engine that runs 16 feasibility checks per flight leg

I designed and built an operations portal around a single idea: the team builds a trip, and the system runs the whole feasibility check for them, per leg, against a unified copy of every source that used to be checked by hand. Each check returns a clear status, and the ones that call for interpretation carry a confidence score so the operator knows how much to trust them.

The checks are organized into four modules. Tap through them.

03The design principle

The human stays in command, and every override is on the record

An automated feasibility check is only trustworthy if a person can overrule it and if the system is honest about what it does not know. Both were first-class from day one.

An operator can bypass a check or force-fail it, and every change is attributed and logged. A read-only workflow history shows every field that moved, old value to new, who changed it, and why. Operations, admin, and super-admin roles see different slices of that trail by design.

When an AI check fails, it gets louder, not quieter

If an AI-assisted check cannot complete, say a source times out, the system does not crash the trip and it does not quietly wave the check through. It converts the failure into Manual Review Required, confidence zero, and the interface renders that state more prominently than a pass.

That is the whole safety model in one rule: a failed check can never silently look like a passed one.

04How it shipped

Architected, built, and delivered into production

I architected the system and personally built it on a managed cloud, while hiring and leading a small delivery team. The engagement started with a round of discovery interviews, moved through an agile delivery cycle, and went live across multiple teams and users, handed over with a developer guide, a user manual, and an operations runbook.

05Architecture

A hub-and-spoke over a unified copy of every source

The operations portal sits at the center. Around it, the four check modules run as spokes, each reading from a local store that a data-ingestion engine keeps in sync with the outside sources on an hourly job, so a trip is evaluated in one place instead of ten.

06Outcome

What changed for the operator

The engine collapsed a manual, hours-long, dozen-system feasibility check into a single reviewed screen, with the human firmly in the loop on anything the system flags. In my experience, that is the kind of change that cuts manual workload and speeds up decisions. I'll publish headline results and nothing under them. A client's cost lines, their contract terms, and the numbers behind any single check aren't mine to put on a public page.

A note on outcomes

What's verifiable in the build is the scope: sixteen checks across four modules, five of them AI-assisted and confidence-scored, over ten-plus unified data sources, with human override and a full audit trail.

07What I took away

Four things I would carry into the next build

  1. Sit with the people before you design anything. The dispatchers carried this whole checklist in their heads. Nobody had written it down, so there was nothing to read. The discovery interviews came before a line of production code, and that is the only reason the sixteen checks match the job instead of matching my idea of the job.
  2. Put wireframes in front of them while the screens are still cheap. Arguing with a picture costs an afternoon. Arguing with a built feature costs a sprint, and by then everyone is defending the thing rather than the job it was for.
  3. You are building for a person, not a role. An operations team is not a job title, it is a handful of people who each work the problem slightly differently. Design against the individual you watched, then check the shape holds for the others. It is a slower start and it saves the rebuild.
  4. Decide out loud what the machine owns and what the person keeps. The hard call was never which model to use. It was which of the checks a human keeps, and where the override sits when the system is confident and wrong. Naming that early is what let the system be handed over instead of babysat.
The judgment was in deciding which checks a machine should make, and which ones a person always signs.
