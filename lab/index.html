The effort lattice

As new models come out, do skills still matter? Does turning up reasoning effort erase what skills add? I ran a pre-registered study on the four publicly available Claude models (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, Fable) at each of their available effort settings.

I pre-registered 17 tasks, with a pass checklist, and a skill library that I use often in two production codebases. Then, at each model and at each effort level, I ran each task cold (with no skills available) and loaded (with the entire skills library available). To avoid noise, I ran each model 3 times, except Fable's medium and xhigh interior levels and the Haiku baseline, and had two judges and one adjudicator.

  • → Skills improved every completed setup, raising the overall average from 88.7% to 98.2%.
  • → Across the dialed models, adding effort without skills improved scores by 3.5 to 4.9 points. Adding skills improved them by 4.4 to 10.4 points on average per model, and by 26.5 points on Haiku in the available run.
  • → Every dialed model using skills at low effort outperformed the same model at high effort without skills.

Agent topologies and the shape of the work

Most leaders meet agentic AI as something to bolt onto the workflow they already run, a faster version of today's process. The bigger move is to redesign the shape of the work around what agents are good at, and that is where the gains compound. What makes it doable is that the shapes you would redesign into are not exotic. They are the ones your organization already runs on, and the most interesting ones might be the shapes it never could.

How the wiring between agents (single, chain, fan-out, or a supervised tree) follows from the shape of the work itself, and what each layout costs you when the work doesn't fit.

Adapted from a course I'm co-developing at MIT on leading agentic AI in organizations. Developed end to end through a fictional call-center billing-dispute case.

  • → How far do we go? Which slices of the work are even candidates for an agent.
  • → How many agents? One agent doing a lot, or many narrow ones handing off.
  • → What blocks what? The dependencies. Where one step cannot run until another clears.
  • → Who needs what context? What each piece is given, and what is too sensitive to hand over.
Every step is the same shape. Something in, something out, riding on the context it has and the data it touches

The skills library

I use Claude Code every day, and these 17 skills are the habits I got tired of re-teaching it. Distilled from two production codebases, they ship as an open instrument with an evaluation harness included. MIT licensed.

A few by name: multi-model adversarial review sets a second vendor's model loose on your own review pass, so no model ever grades its own work; the systematic debugging playbook makes you pin down ground truth before you reach for a hypothesis; and the pre-merge validation gate fixes what "done" has to mean before a change is allowed to land.

The evidence behind it: an order-blinded A/B across 50 pre-registered must-hits in 16 tasks. Baseline, 26 of 50. With the library, 46 of 50. On the regulated-data consent task, which has four must-hits, the loaded agent caught all four on every run; the cold agent usually did too, but slipped to three on some runs.

How it's built

01 Adversarial review Attacked before it is trusted

An independent review pass reads the work fresh and argues against it, and refuses sign-off until the strongest objection against the work has been answered.

02 Tiered escalation Finished work climbs before it lands

A finished deliverable climbs a ladder, the same way it would inside a real firm. Work that fails a level goes back down with specific feedback, not a vague rejection, and climbs again.

03 Evidence harness Settled by evidence, not opinion

Whether a change ships is settled against fixed test cases, against what is already running in production, and against reviewers who do not know which answer they are grading.

04 Security controls Built for production, not a demo

The systems we build carry their own controls, not bolted on after the fact, from tamper-evident audit hash chains and prompt-injection guards to human-approval gates.
