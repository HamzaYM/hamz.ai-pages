A field guide / agentic systems

Designing
agentic systems

Most teams meet agentic AI as something to bolt onto the process they already run. The bigger move is to redesign the shape of the work around what agents are good at. That is where the gains compound.

Worked end to end on one call Adapted from a course I co-developed at MIT By Hamza Malik
01The reframe

Not everything should be an LLM

A leader owns a call center and knows it cold. He goes to a summit on agentic AI and comes home looking at his operation with new eyes. The reflex is to automate the workflow he already has, a faster version of today. That is the smaller prize.

The larger move is to redesign how the work is structured. Before any of that, one thing has to be true: you have to know which parts of the work actually need a model, and which parts are plain code wearing a costume.

The reflex

Automate what he already has

Point a model at the current process. Faster, cheaper, familiar. The wins are real but they cap out fast, because the shape of the work never changed.

The reframe

Redesign the shape of the work

Ask what agents are actually good at, then restructure around it. Harder, and where the compounding gains live. The rest of this guide is how you make that call.

Put the model exactly where judgment is needed, and use plain code everywhere else.

03What is actually a model

A call is a stack, and most of it is not an LLM

A call is a stack of different parts, and only some of them are the reasoning model. The caller's voice becomes text through a speech-to-text model. The reply becomes voice through a text-to-speech model. Neither of those is the LLM. Verifying the caller is a database lookup. Posting the refund is an API call. None of that is an LLM either.

Hover each layer. Only one of them reasons.

Reaching for an LLM to look up a balance or play a hold message is the same mistake as spawning fifty agents for a one-line question.

04The shapes

Five common shapes, and what each one costs

There is no settled vocabulary here. Microsoft ships five orchestration patterns, OpenAI splits the work into a manager pattern and handoffs, LangChain renamed its own set between 2024 and 2026. So this guide uses plain words on purpose. These are five common shapes from a much larger set. Each one is scored on the same four gauges: how long the caller waits, how many tokens a run burns, how much gets checked before anything commits, and how contained the data stays.

Hub and spoke
orchestrator + narrow workers
+ Fast, parallel dispatch.
− Nothing checks the workers, so one wrong answer can pass straight through.
On the call: a coordinator fires the balance lookup, the account agent, and the knowledge agent at once. The shape Anthropic used for its research product.
Pyramid
hierarchical, with review
+ Catches mistakes before they commit.
− Slow and costly. A reviewer sits above the base and a final agent signs off.
On the call: base agents pull amount, policy, and history, a reviewer checks, a final agent signs off before money moves.
Pipeline
sequential assembly line
+ Predictable and the easiest shape to audit. Every handoff is a clean gate.
− Slow, and the whole chain stalls if any one step jams.
On the call: the after-call wrap-up. Transcript to summary to disposition code to follow-up task.
Parallel
concurrent, independent
+ The fastest shape. The whole bet is independence.
− Turns dangerous when the branches secretly do depend, and you reconcile mismatched results.
On the call: identity lookup, sentiment, and intent classification the instant a call lands.
Mesh
peer to peer, no boss
+ Flexible. Bounded, it is a capped group chat of about three agents.
− Hard to govern, which is why you keep a supervisor watching. Use rarely.
On the call: a fraud agent and a save-the-customer agent negotiating a retention offer.

05Cost, speed, rigor

The reviewer is cheaper than it looks

A lot of what an agent reads is identical on every single call. The refund policy does not change between callers. The product catalog is the same at 9am and 9pm. Caching means you process that stable context once and reuse it, instead of paying to reread it every time.

A rep does not relearn the refund policy on every call. It is in the binder on the desk. Caching is that binder. Toggle below to see what stays in the binder and what has to be read fresh for each caller.

• Fresh · per caller

  • The live conversation

The pyramid looks expensive until you remember the policy its reviewer checks against is cached. Caching does not change which shape you pick, only what it costs to run.

06Containment

A privacy wall, not a third org chart

One agent is the only thing that ever speaks to the customer, and it is deliberately starved of sensitive context. A second agent holds the full picture inside and passes outward only what is safe to say. The external agent cannot leak what it never held. If you handle data-residency rules or health records, this is the feature you want.

07Escalation and approval

Gate only the writes that matter

A human sits at a checkpoint. Before a high-stakes action commits, a refund over a limit, an account closed, it pauses for a person to approve, edit, or reject. It is the cheapest insurance on the actions that sit on the far side of the read/write line. Reading an account is low risk. Changing it is not. Risk piles up the moment an agent can write to a system of record.

Human in the loop

The person is the orchestrator, the way a head chef calls the line. Nothing moves without them. Best when a mistake is costly and each decision needs a human hand.

Human on the loop

The person supervises an autonomous orchestrator, keeping control of objectives and oversight rather than of each step. More reach, but you carry the accountability harder.

The rule

A refund over $500 pauses for a rep. A refund under the limit posts on its own. The escalation specialist is the only agent that holds the authority to offer a refund or a retention credit, so the routine agents never default to giving money away.

Nobody approves a balance lookup.

08Adversarial review

A checker you can bolt onto any shape

One agent does the work and a second agent checks it. The checker has no job except to find what is wrong. It is often the same model run again with fresh instructions and no memory of the first draft, so it has nothing to defend. It catches mistakes the first agent talked itself into.

Reach for it when you need to trust what the agent did, anything that moves money or goes out under your name. Skip it on a password reset.

In the field

At Casper, Sierra runs supervisors that review each response in parallel and escalate to a human when needed. The deployment resolved 74% of inquiries and lifted CSAT by 20 points. The checker is the mechanism, not the marketing.

09The evidence, and its edges

More agents win, until they do not

When Anthropic rebuilt its research product as a lead agent coordinating several specialists in parallel, it scored far higher than a single agent, and paid for that gain with far more tokens. The pattern is real, and so is the boundary.

The edge is dependency. Domains that need every agent to share the same context, or that carry many sequential dependencies, are not a good fit for multi-agent systems today. Google's numbers show the same shape degrading 39 to 70 percent on sequential tasks. Cognition makes the counter-case outright in Don't Build Multi-Agents: context fragments, decisions disperse, and coding is the clearest place it breaks.

10What this guide sets aside

Four real constraints, named on purpose

The shapes above are the design vocabulary. Four things sit outside it and decide, in practice, which shapes you can actually reach.

01

Live-call latency

A pyramid review is realistically a post-call or asynchronous pattern. You do not make a caller wait on a chain of reviewers.

02

Compliance in regulated settings

Call-recording consent, PCI scope for anything that touches card data, and dispute-handling rules are design inputs, not afterthoughts. In a regulated center they decide where the wall goes and which agent is allowed to write.

03

Vendor platform versus greenfield

The platform you are already on constrains which shapes are actually reachable. Ambition meets the tooling you have.

04

The workforce transition

The reps' work concentrates rather than disappears. The dull, dirty, and dangerous leaves. The judgment and the accountability stay with a person.

11Reading the numbers

Ask what the metric means before you believe it

Deflection and resolution are not standardized terms. Every vendor defines them differently, so ask for the definition before you accept the number. A system that deflects a call by frustrating the caller into hanging up scores the same as one that actually solved the problem.

The cautionary arc

Klarna's assistant launched loud: 2.3 million conversations, resolution time from 11 minutes to under 2. Then in May 2025 came the reversal. Cost had been made too dominant a factor, quality slipped, and the company began rehiring humans.

Citi took the other road. Its Agent Assist is a human-in-the-loop copilot across more than 5,000 reps and 11 million card calls, run on the stated principle that they cannot afford anything going wrong with any interaction.

12Putting it together

The shapes nest

A system does not have to take one shape. On the same billing-dispute call, the shapes compose. You do not pick just one.

  1. A privacy wall sits on the outside, so the customer-facing agent never holds sensitive context.
  2. Inside, parallel branches run the moment the call lands: identity, sentiment, intent.
  3. Hub and spoke gathers the account details across systems.
  4. A pyramid gives the money back, because real funds move and someone has to sign off.
  5. A pipeline handles the after-call wrap-up, from transcript to disposition to follow-up.

These shapes are not exotic. They are how your organization already works: the delegating manager, the firm with layers of review, the front office and back office, the standard operating procedure, the task force. Galbraith read structure as information processing, Mintzberg cataloged the shapes, and equifinality says different designs can reach the same end. There is rarely one best structure.

There is no best shape. There is only the right shape for this call.
